# Free-Rider Tracker
### Problem Validation & Research
*NYC CodeQuest — Batch 4 Final Round | Track: HUMAN — Engineer for Human Behavior*

---

## 1. Problem Statement (as given)

> People do not always behave the way systems expect. They forget, misunderstand, ignore instructions, take shortcuts, make mistakes, and adapt systems to their own habits.
>
> **Goal:** Identify a real-world situation where human behavior causes a system to fail or become inefficient, and engineer a solution that works with human behavior rather than assuming humans will change it.

---

## 2. The Real-World Situation We're Targeting

Group work — in classrooms, hackathons, and workplaces — runs on one quiet assumption: that every member will honestly report what they contributed. Grading, credit, and evaluation are built on top of that self-report.

That assumption fails constantly. It fails not because tools are missing (peer evaluation forms, task boards, and check-in bots already exist), but because self-report is friction, and friction is exactly what people route around under time pressure or low accountability. The result is the **free-rider problem**: one or more members contribute far less than their share, while credit is split evenly.

---

## 3. Why This Happens — What the Research Shows

- **Free-riding is not a rare edge case.** A survey of 205 students across all faculties at an Australian university found free-riding was the single greatest concern students raised about group work, across every discipline.
- **It is usually not deliberate laziness.** The same research found free-riding behavior often stems from varied underlying causes — confusion, conflicting schedules, low confidence — rather than a simple unwillingness to work.
- **It is measurably tied to personality traits that self-report can't see or correct for:** studies using the Big Five model found agreeableness and conscientiousness were significant negative predictors of social loafing — i.e., the students most likely to loaf are also the least likely to be caught by a system that depends on people reporting on themselves or each other.
- **Timing matters:** research on peer-assessment design found that early, frequent, and specific evaluation criteria reduced free-riding far more than a single end-of-project rating — but most real classroom and workplace tools only evaluate once, at the end, when it's too late to fix.
- **The cost isn't just fairness on paper** — it's the single most-cited source of frustration in group work: students report receiving the same grade as teammates who produced a fraction of the output.

---

## 4. Existing Solutions & Their Gaps

Every existing mitigation still relies on someone choosing to self-report, at some point, honestly. That's the load-bearing weakness across all of them:

| Approach | How it works today | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| **Peer evaluation forms** | Teammates rate each other's contribution, usually once, at the end of the project. | Subjective, filled in after the fact, easy to game with reciprocal high ratings, and too late to fix anything. |
| **Task boards** (Trello/Asana/Jira) | Members manually create and check off tasks as done. | Requires everyone to remember to log work — the exact self-report step that free-riders skip. |
| **Instructor / manager spot-checks** | A supervisor periodically asks the team for a status update. | Doesn't scale past a handful of teams, and only samples a moment in time. |
| **Status check-in bots** | A Slack/Teams bot pings each member to report progress. | Adds a new habit to adopt — exactly the kind of friction that gets ignored first. |

---

## 5. Our Solution

Instead of asking anyone to log, report, or check in, the **Free-Rider Tracker** reconstructs true contribution from records that already exist as a side effect of doing the work — GitHub commit history, Google Docs/Notion edit history, and similar activity trails. Members link their accounts once; from that point on, the system observes rather than asks.

At submission time, it auto-detects the group from shared repos/docs and generates a **Group Insight Report**: a contribution breakdown per member, a timeline of when work actually happened, and plain-language insights (e.g., who carried the project in its final hours vs. throughout).

---

## 6. What Makes It Unique

| Dimension | Existing tools | Free-Rider Tracker |
|---|---|---|
| **Data source** | Self-report (forms, check-ins, task boards) | Passive activity already generated by real work (commits, doc edits, timestamps) |
| **Adoption friction** | High — a new habit every member must keep up | Near zero — nothing new to log; the system reads what already exists |
| **Timing** | End-of-project or periodic snapshots | Continuous — reconstructed at any point from the full history |
| **Bias** | Subjective, socially influenced, reciprocity-gameable | Objective and independently verifiable from source platforms |
| **Design assumption** | Assumes people will change behavior and report honestly | Assumes they won't, and designs around it instead |

---

## 7. Why This Fits the HUMAN Track

The brief explicitly warns against assuming humans will change their habits. Every existing free-riding fix — forms, check-ins, task boards — makes exactly that assumption: it asks people to behave differently (report honestly, log consistently) and fails when they don't. The Free-Rider Tracker never asks anyone to change anything. It treats self-report as unreliable by design and reads the one signal that isn't: the work itself, as it was actually done.

## 8. Validation Plan

- Run the tracker on our own hackathon repository during the event — our own commit history becomes a live, unfaked proof that the system produces an accurate contribution picture, with no staged demo required.
- Cross-check the generated report against our own team's honest self-assessment of who did what, to sanity-check the scoring logic in real time.
